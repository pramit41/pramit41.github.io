After spending a couple of days in Kathmandu, we headed south to the Terai region, toward Chitwan and Bara. Along the way, I saw vast farmlands and grazing cattle—scenery the region is known for. In Bara, I walked through fields of corn and even picked a few to take home. Unlike Kathmandu, where houses are stacked close together, here they were spread far apart, separated by farmland that gave a sense of space and privacy. Later, we visited relatives in the area who welcomed us warmly with sweet, triangular-shaped roti and homemade yogurt for lunch. Through them, I got a glimpse of everyday rural life. Most families here were subsistence farmers with hens, cows, buffalo, and goats. Adults spent their days tending to the land and animals, while children went to school and helped around the house. This rhythm of life was something I noticed repeated in other rural areas I visited as well.

It was outside Kathmandu where I also came to see how deeply cultural practices shape Nepali society. In Chitwan, my family and I attended Kul Puja, a Hindu event where offerings are made to one’s ancestors. Since many of our relatives we hadn’t seen in years were attending, it felt like the perfect occasion to join. When we arrived in the morning, hundreds of people were already gathered at the temple—many having traveled from outside Chitwan. After the prayers, the organizers collected donations and began preparing food for everyone. What struck me most was how this full-day event wasn’t only about worship; it brought relatives together, strengthened community bonds, and raised money for future cultural and social efforts.

To make the most of our time, we visited other religious sites in Chitwan as well. One temple that was located near a river, revealed just how wide-reaching these institutions are. We offered our prayers while keeping an eye on mischievous monkeys circling for food offerings, then spoke with a temple member. He explained that the temple not only taught Sanskrit to young adults preparing to become priests, but also housed and fed elderly people with no one else to care for them. In that moment, I realized how faith in Nepal extends beyond spiritual practice—it provides education, community, and a safety net. Religion here isn’t separate from society; it is woven directly into it.
Another memorable stop was Gorkha, where my cousin’s Toyota Hilux carried us along a rugged road that at times forced us out of the car to lighten the load. Around noon, we reached a small village that seemed to have more dogs and cattle than people. A family friend greeted us with refreshing mohi (buttermilk) and a traditional Nepali meal that included wild chicken—a special request from my cousin. After lunch, we walked to a nearby mango grove that my dad explained held great importance to our ancestors. Stretching as far as I could see, the evenly spaced trees once produced enough fruit to feed an entire village. Today, with fewer people around to care for them, the grove has thinned, though the history remains. At its center stood a small, damaged temple built by my ancestors. We paused there, took some photos, and then continued on toward our main destination: Kyamuntar.

After another hour, we reached Kyamuntar, where we met my dad’s uncle, a local legend known for killing tigers many years ago. Though he is now very old and his eyesight fading, I noticed a tiger tooth hanging from his necklace—proof that the stories were true. After catching up, we began the hike to my dad’s old home. With no wide road leading up the hill, the only option was to go on foot. A stray dog even joined us along the way, as if guiding us toward the house.

My dad’s childhood home was the last one up the hill. Tall grass had grown around it, and one side was badly damaged from the 2015 earthquake. We took photos in front of it, and my aunt filmed short videos reminiscing about her childhood. Behind the house, we set up a small picnic with the snacks we had carried—also sharing some with our loyal canine companion. As I sat there, I took in the breathtaking view: layers of green hills in the distance, farmland scattered below, and the Budhigandaki River winding its way through the valley. I had only been here once before, as a first grader, so I had no real memory of it. Seeing it again now, I felt grateful to connect with the place where my dad and his siblings grew up. I don’t know when I’ll return, but I hope one day to come back for an even bigger picnic—this time with more of my relatives.
